# Moreton Island · Living Map

A living 3D map of Moreton Island (Mulgumpin), Queensland. Drag to move, right-drag or two fingers to turn, scroll or pinch to zoom.

- Real relief from the Queensland Government laser survey (bare ground), draped with aerial photography; sharp photo tiles stream in near the view
- The real surveyed seabed under see-through water, (chart-style depth lines can be switched on with the Depths button), swell, foam, sun glitter and cloud reflections
- 550 real building outlines, the 4WD tracks and walking paths, the Tangalooma and Bulwer wrecks, Cape Moreton lighthouse, the resort jetty, beacons and buoys
- Yachts and cruisers at anchor that swing to the wind, yachts under sail, and the island ferries on their charted routes
- Drifting clouds with shadows, sun, moon (true phase) and stars for the real time of day
- Live weather, swell and tide from Open-Meteo, or set your own with sliders (sunshine, cloud cover and type, wind, rain, fog, swell, tide, lightning)
- Walk mode: press Walk (or double-click a spot) to explore on foot with keyboard and mouse, touch or a gamepad. On a touch screen, a finger on the view walks forward for as long as it is down; slide it sideways to steer or far up to run, and put two fingers down to stand still and look around
- The real plants of the island, modelled from photographs taken there: crooked scribbly gums and bloodwoods with mottled bark and tufts of hanging leaves on many twigs, multi-stemmed casuarinas with drooping tassels, gnarled banksias, paperbarks, mangroves at the tide line, grass trees with black trunks, brown skirts and tall spikes, dense twiggy scrub, pandanus on the foredunes and coconut palms at the resort and villages. Some country is burnt, with charred trunks
- The floor of the bush is sand, the same pale yellow as the beaches, with dark detritus on it: drifts of dead leaves, bark, sticks and charcoal under the trees, and almost no grass
- The low wet flats (as east of Bulwer) are fern country: a green carpet of ferns under dark, leaning paperbarks and gums, many of them scorched or standing dead
- Township houses in the island's styles: small fibro and weatherboard beach shacks, highset houses on stumps with a verandah and steps, newer two-storey places with skillion roofs, tin sheds and the odd shipping container, all under ribbed corrugated iron, with rainwater tanks and solar panels (the townships are off the grid)
- Textured buildings and cars: weatherboards, fibro sheets with battens, rendered block, grooved cladding and corrugated iron on the walls; framed windows with panes, blinds and sills, doors, lit rooms at night; ribbed iron roofs with screw lines and rust. The 4WDs are smooth lofted bodies (bonnet, raked windscreen, cabin that leans in above the belt line) on rounded tyres, with a bull bar and roof rack of tube and a swag on top; glass, door seams and handles, grille, number plates, steel rims and beach dust are painted on
- Cape Moreton: the sandstone lighthouse with its two red bands, the keepers' white cottages where the map has them, wind-shorn banksias leaning away from the sea wind, long grass
- Life: shells, sea stars and driftwood on the sand, 4WDs and their wheel ruts on the beach highways, snorkelers at the wrecks, schools of fish around every wreck, green turtles and stingrays under the water; gulls, crested terns, pelicans and cormorants (afloat by the resort jetty too), whistling and brahminy kites, sea eagles and ospreys, pied oystercatchers; blue tiger, common crow, common brown and grass-blue butterflies, and dragonflies over the low wet country
- The Bulwer wrecks: three hulks rusted down to red-brown shells and ribs, standing along the hull outlines in the map data (two across the beach, the third along the shore, awash)
- Tides: the sea rises and falls with the clock (a harmonic model sized for Moreton Bay, shifted to the live sea level when online; the Tide slider overrides it). Low water bares wet, rippled sand: wider beaches and the sandbanks of the south-west. Boats left by the tide sit on the sand
- Sea life: every animal is a smooth, lofted body with curved fins, painted per pixel (countershading, scales, bars and spots, an eye, fin rays, surface light rippling over its back). Damselfish of the Queensland coast crowd the wrecks: Indo-Pacific sergeants, neon damsels and humbugs, with bream circling wider. Round the island there are schools of baitfish and whiting, flathead on the sand, bluespotted rays (more of them in the shallows at low tide), shovelnose rays, small whaler sharks, green turtles, bottlenose dolphins (a pod stays off the Tangalooma jetty), dugongs on the seagrass banks, humpback whales off Cape Moreton, swarms of jellyfish, and soldier crabs on the flats
- People: walkers on the township streets and beaches, people fishing at the water's edge in front of each town, and small camps dotted behind the beaches (dome tents, a parked 4WD, a campfire that glows at night)
- Signposts where tracks meet (and nowhere else): one pointed board for each way on, naming the places that way leads to and how far they are along the tracks and beaches. Green boards for vehicle tracks, brown for walking tracks, blue along the beach
- More on the water: flybridge cruisers at anchor and under way along the west coast, and jet skis carving about off Tangalooma
- In the bush: clouds of butterflies and midges, wallabies resting and hopping in the scrub, goannas walking the sand and small skinks that sit, then dart
- Under water: flathead lying on the sand in the lagoons and sheltered shallows, schools of whiting in the shallows, and in deeper water patches of rock grown over with weed and kelp, with sea cucumbers on the sand around them
- Diving: walk into the sea until you float, then swim forward while looking down to go under (look up to come back; C sinks, Space rises). Under water the view is framed by a diving mask, breaths of bubbles rise past the glass, the water hazes blue-green with distance, and the surface overhead is a rippling window onto the sky
- The Tangalooma wrecks are single skins of near-black rust with ribs standing inside, their top edges eaten ragged and holes rusted through. Under water they are reefs: turf algae, pink coralline crust, orange sponge and barnacles over the rust
- Ride along: tap any moving 4WD and you sit in its back seat as it drives the beach. The cabin is drawn round the view (roof lining, sun visors, mirror, pillars and side windows, dashboard with a line of stitching, a hat left on the dash, the wheel on the right), the screen on the dash shows the speed, the heading, which beach it is and the nearest named place; drag to look about, and Leave the car (or Esc) puts you back above it
- From the owner's photographs of the south and south-west: the beaches of the bay side are white (the ocean beaches a pale yellow); a dark, wavering line of seagrass wrack lies at the water's edge with an older line at the last high water and scraps between; the shallows are clear, the sand showing its own colour knee deep, with near-black seagrass beds; pelicans stand in groups on the sand spits facing the wind, most of them toward Kooringal and the banks by the Big Sandhills; the beach highways carry many overlapping wheel ruts; thin cirrus streaks the sky

- Tree shadows that follow the sun: every tree near you casts the shadow of its crown and trunk on the ground. The shadows are long in the morning and evening, short at noon, swing round through the day, and sunlight comes through the leaves in moving flecks (the ground works out where each tree stands the same way the trees do, so no shadow map is needed)

- More from the owner's photographs: fallen trees, bleached silver, lie on the eroding dune edges and the back of the beaches, root plate at one end and bare limbs at the other; the seagrass beds lie in long dark bands along the shore; rows of weathered timber bollards mark the track mouths by the beach signs; on the steep faces of the Desert and the sandhills the sand has slid in tongues, leaving streaks straight down the slope and darker damp patches; the Big and Little Sandhills are paler, nearly white, with smoky wisps of dark mineral sand and clean bright drifts; at the water's edge the bright dry sand ends at a clean wandering line and the wet sand below it is warm tan; a belt of dune grass (green tussocks and older ones gone to straw) stands between the open beach and the first trees; behind the grass, patches of bracken fern, fresh green ones and brown ones where fire has been through; goat's-foot vine creeps out from the foot of the scrub with pink and yellow flowers; the two hulls charted on the bay beach north of the Big Sandhills now lie at the water's edge along the shore, a long low run of rusted side with a tall stern post
- Inside the 4WD: woven roof lining, grained dash with a stitched edge and speaker grille, door tops with pulls and switches, wipers at rest on the glass, a stitched leather wheel that turns as the car turns (and spins back as it straightens), perforated leather seats. While you ride, the weather panel and the control bar get out of the way
- Travel menu: a small Travel chip under the title opens a list (4WD, Boat, Yacht, Jet ski, Pelican, Sea eagle). Each has two buttons: Watch goes along with the nearest one while it looks after itself; Drive, Steer, Sail, Ride or Fly puts you in control of it. Press the same button again to move on to the next one of that kind; Back to the map leaves

- Steady travel: the eye is placed after everything has moved each frame, so the deck of a boat or the bird ahead of you never jumps about; hulls have weight (a ferry rises and falls slowly, a dinghy quickly) instead of following every ripple; the bird camera swings round and rises after the bird; 4WDs take bends and turn round at the end of the beach smoothly. In the 4WD you can look all the way round: the rear doors, the load-space windows, the back window with the spare wheel beyond it, and the head rests of your own seat. Sea eagles ride the rising air up to a couple of hundred metres and slide back down to the treetops; flown by hand they go from just above the water to nearly 600 m. The windows of the 4WD are big all round (tall windscreen and door windows, thin pillars), the flybridge hardtop is high enough to see out under, and stepping into or out of a ride the view comes up out of the dark instead of cutting. Watch from outside: scroll, pinch, press - or use the Further button and the eye leaves the seat and follows the 4WD, boat or bird from behind and above, as far back as you like (drag to swing round it); Closer brings you back aboard. Birds stay above the treetops and often go much higher; the sea eagle is its own bird (grey, broad fingered wings held in a V, white head and tail). The cruiser's helm view is from the coachroof ahead of the wheelhouse, the yacht's from the rail clear of the sail, the jet ski's from just behind the rider. A hand-driven 4WD will not climb a dune face head on and can only crawl in the bush

- Wakes: every moving boat leaves a spreading V of broken foam from the shoulders of its bow (wider for a slow hull, narrower for a planing one), with boiling white wash straight astern that thins to streaks, and curls a sheet of spray off each side of the bow; a jet ski also throws up the plume of its jet

- The plane: a high-wing light aircraft waits at one of the island's two airstrips (marked with cones and a windsock), takes off, tours the island a place at a time, joins for the other strip, lands, turns round and waits again. Go along with it (through the windscreen, close behind or far behind) or fly it yourself: bank, climb, and put it down on a strip

- The traveller: someone with a pack, a rolled mat and a walking pole, going round every township, camping area, lookout, lake and named place on the island (34 of them) along the tracks and beaches, nearest first. At dark, or when you press Camp, they pitch a dome tent and sit down beside it; Break camp sends them on. Walk along with them or walk them yourself

- Tour them all: one button in the Travel menu takes each kind of travel in turn (4WD, boat, yacht, jet ski, pelican, sea eagle, plane, traveller) for the time you choose (15 s to 5 min each), a third of it through the eyes, a third from close behind and a third from far behind

- Somewhere different every time: each time the map is opened the jet skis, small boats, cruisers and yachts are dealt out afresh over the waters right round the island, the traveller sets out from a different place and the plane from either strip; and asking the Travel menu (or the tour) for a kind of travel takes you to one in a different part of the island from the last few, not always the nearest. No figures are shown aboard the boats

- Vehicles in curves: deckhouses have rounded corners, solid pillars between the panes and crowned roofs; hardtops and platforms are rounded slabs; the jet ski is a sculpted cowl and saddle with handlebars, grips, mirrors and a low screen; the dinghy's outboard has a rounded cowl and tiller; the plane's wing has a round leading edge and its skin shows panel seams, rivet lines and exhaust soot; cabin sides show panel joints and rain streaks

- Sea eagles at rest: a pair sits in a bare dead tree at five places (by the Little and Big Sandhills, near the wrecks, on the cape and at Kooringal), upright, white head and breast, grey wings folded, as in the owner's photo

- Clear airstrips: both strips (Tangalooma and Kooringal) are broad cleared lanes that run on well past each end, with no tree, bush or fern on them, so the plane takes off and lands over open ground

- People and animals with real bodies (src/bodies.js): each is built from smooth limbs with true proportions and painted per pixel. People have a torso, neck, head with nose and ears, hair, arms with hands, legs that bend at the knee as they walk, and feet; an untucked shirt (plain, hooped or checked), shorts (some with a flower print) or trousers, bare feet, thongs or shoes, a straw hat on some, and a face (eyes, brows, mouth, a beard on some). They vary: men, women (narrower shoulders, wider hips, some with long hair) and children. The swamp wallaby is dark grey-brown above and rusty below with black paws, tail tip and face; the lace monitor is near black with rows of cream dots and a banded tail; the blue-tongue is a fat fawn lizard with dark bars. Birds reworked too: the pied cormorant is the right size with a white throat and face; pelicans at rest show the black folded wing and stand breast-up on slate legs; flying pelicans have broad fingered wings; sea eagles are grey-backed and bulkier when perched; soldier crabs are smaller with cream claws

- Headlamps at night: each 4WD under way throws two dipped beams that light the sand, the tracks, the grass and ferns, tree trunks and people ahead of it, widening as they go and fading out by about a hundred metres, with a faint shaft of light in the air in front of the car. From the back seat you see the lit ground ahead through the windscreen. The traveller's Break camp works at any hour: the tent comes down at once and they walk on through the dark, camping by themselves again the next evening

- Clean golden beaches: the sand is a clear, warm yellow everywhere (a touch paler on the bay side), with only the gentlest variation. The grey patches, dark dune streaks, smoky mineral wisps and scattered weed that had crept in are gone or reduced to a hint; wet sand is a deeper gold, and there is just a thin faint line of weed at the water's edge

- Beaches at every tide: the sea follows the clock between a spring low (about -1.15 m) and a spring high (about +1.05 m), and there is always beach left at the top of the tide. Measured on the map's own ground: the bay beaches have about 20 m of dry sand at high water, 30 m at mid tide and 65 to 75 m at low water; the ocean beach about 60 m, 80 m and 165 to 175 m. How much each stretch widens depends on the slope of its own seabed. When online the real hour-by-hour tide is used, but the sea level is never shifted up or down to match a reading (that once held it near high water all day). Changing the time hands the tide back to the clock if the Tide slider had been holding it. Dune grass, creeper and ferns keep to the foot of the dunes, above the reach of the sea, so the beach itself is open sand

- Feathers, scales and skin: birds are feathered per pixel (rows of small body feathers each lapping the next, long flight feathers with a shaft and barbs, scaly legs, streaked bills) on the pelicans afloat, ashore and in flight, the sea eagles and the cormorant. Fish show countable scales, each its own shade and some flashing silver as the fish turns, a lateral line, a mouth, a round pupil in a gold ring with a glint, and fins with rays and thin membranes. Dolphins carry tooth-rake scars, an eye line and a blowhole; dugongs a film of algae, pale scars and muzzle bristles; humpbacks head knobs, barnacle patches and long scratches; sharks a sandpaper grain

- More finish: foliage stands in clumps at the branch ends with sky between, lit on top and dark beneath, on thicker trunks; buildings sit on the highest ground under their walls and throw shadows; birds and the plane cast a shadow that slides away as they climb; lines of footprints wander over dry sand; the surf's white water has a torn edge; the jet ski's plume is a thin broken stream; perched eagles shift and look about; mangroves are asked to stand in the shallows of the sheltered south-west shore; on a phone the Travel list folds away once you choose and the hint fades after a few seconds
- Sea eagles: white-bellied sea eagles soar in slow circles high over the headlands and wooded hills, hardly beating a wing. Follow one or fly it
- Fly a pelican: Australian pelicans glide in lines low over the water off each township and the wrecks, a few slow beats and then a long glide. Tap one to follow just behind it; choose Fly it yourself to steer, climb and sink, bring it down onto the water (it folds its wings and paddles) and take off again
- Drive: in a 4WD, Take the wheel steers it anywhere on the island (not into the sea) with the arrow buttons or A/D and W/S; hand it back near the beach and it picks up its run again, or leave it parked where you stopped. A jet ski is yours to ride as soon as you are on it
- Go aboard: tap any yacht, cruiser, jet ski or ferry and you stand at its helm as it moves with the swell. Watch it sail itself, or take the helm (button or H): steer with the arrow buttons or A/D, ease off or add speed with − and + or S/W. A yacht steered too close to the wind stalls; any boat takes the ground in the shallows. Instruments show speed in knots, heading, wind, depth under you and the nearest named place. Boats at anchor and the ferries can be watched but not steered
- Fresh tyre marks: every moving 4WD lays two ribbons of tread behind it that fade over a couple of minutes and wash out near the water
- Textured boats: raked bows, shaped deckhouses with framed windows, laid timber decks with grain, non-skid on painted decks, bottom paint with weed and barnacles, sails with stitched panels, battens, a class badge and sail numbers, and sunlight off the water playing over the topsides. Each kind of hull has its own paintwork: yachts carry a boot stripe, a gold cove line and round ports that light up at night; motor cruisers a sweeping sheer stripe and oblong ports; the aluminium dinghy pressed strakes, ribs and scratches; the ferries welded plates, rust weeping from the scuppers and draught marks. All carry registration figures on the bow, weep streaks, jetty scrapes and a tide stain. Fitted out with rub rails, stanchions and lifelines, pulpits, standing rigging, a wheel, winches, hatches, spray hood, anchors, fenders, a radar dome and an ensign
- Surf: on the open beaches the swell stands up into waves with a long back and a steep, green-lit face, which tilt to the light as they roll in, break white at the crest and trail a net of foam
- Sound (the Sound button): wind and leaves, ocean surf and bay ripples, rain and thunder, cicadas by day, crickets by night, and the calls of local birds, all made in the browser and following where you are, the weather and the hour
- The ground is one smooth surface: a coarse mesh covers the island and finer grids follow the view, so dunes stay rounded up close and trees stand on the ground
- Every panel can be minimised to a small named chip with its – button, and opened again with +
- Download: the Download button (or `?download` added to the address) saves the whole map as one HTML file of about 37 MB that opens on its own, even offline

Run locally: serve this folder with any static web server (for example `python -m http.server`) and open it in a browser.

- Townships with the critic: tanks only go where there is room between neighbours; roofs have fascia and barge boards and a ridge cap, in weathered zinc, grey-green and old red, with rust starting at the eaves and sheet laps; verandahs have balustrades and open timber stairs with treads and a handrail, and sliding glass doors behind them; highset houses stand a storey up on stumps with batten screens beneath; rainwater tanks are round, ribbed and lidded
- Yards: garden trees (gum, she-oak, banksia, paperbark) stand between the houses and shade them, patchy lawn grows round them, and beside many houses there is a parked 4WD, a rotary clothes hoist with washing out, or a tinny on its trailer
- Round the houses: low ankle-high grass in patches and drifts of fallen leaves, with worn sand between, both on the ground and as single blades and leaves up close
- Third round with the critic: gum leaves hang in distinct sprays, each spray turned its own way, with thin places between and the odd glossy leaf catching the light; she-oaks are grey-green; town trees are tall gums that stand over the roofs; far hills are dark olive with sunlit crown tops and a ragged skyline; fewer two-storey houses; low houses show their stumps in shade under the walls; not every bay has a window and the glass shows the sky; the Cape Moreton light is white with two red bands; low plants and ground litter no longer fall in rows
- People's shadows are their own shape, in the same pose and stride, laid on the ground away from the sun; people stand in the town yards (by the washing, the car, the boat), often two together
- She-oaks (casuarinas) in the two forms the owner photographed: at the back of the beach a low tree whose limbs reach out and droop over the sand, hung with plumes of fine needles; behind it and through the forest a taller one with several thin dark stems that climb steeply, an open see-through crown and a tuft of long drooping needles at every twig end. The needles are fine staggered strands up close and soft downward streaks from further off
- People sit as well as stand: in camp chairs by the campfires and in the town yards
- Under the scrub the ground is all but covered with fallen leaves: a brown carpet, old leaves dark and matted beneath, single leaves on top in every shade from red-brown to pale tan, with only chinks of sand showing; the open beach and dunes stay clean
- Long green grass grows thick round the foot of every tall she-oak
- Bay-side beach sand (Tangalooma, Bulwer, Kooringal) is the pale warm beige of the owner's photograph; the ocean side and the sandhills stay more golden; wheel tracks on the beaches show a little more
- Wallabies are few
- Fourth round with the critic: leaves are drawn at their true size (a hand long) whatever the size of the clump, and lie every way round on the top of a crown, so crowns seen from the air are dappled, not striped; trees stand in groves with open ground between; limbs bow less and trunk feet no longer swell; far crowns sit closer in tone to the canopy; wheel-track driveways run from the street to each parked car; gas bottles stand at the back wall; washing is in smaller pieces; fallen logs are shorter; a wading person's shadow no longer lies on the water

Relief is exaggerated 1.7 times and the seabed comes from a 30 m survey grid refined with water colour, so this is a picture of the island, not a navigation chart.

`tools/fetch.py` and `tools/fetch_qld.py` download the source data, `tools/build_data.py` and `tools/gen_textures.py` turn it into the files in `assets/`.
